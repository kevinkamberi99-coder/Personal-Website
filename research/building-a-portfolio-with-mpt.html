Building a Portfolio with Modern Portfolio Theory

Published22 Sep 2026
Length13 pages
CategoryPortfolio Management
Download PDF

Abstract

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) remains the foundational framework for building diversified investment portfolios, more than seventy years after Harry Markowitz introduced it. This paper reviews the theoretical basis for MPT — the risk-return trade-off, diversification, and the Efficient Frontier — before turning to how institutional investors extend the framework in practice through the Black-Litterman model, factor diversification, and disciplined risk management. It closes with an illustrative strategic portfolio built using J.P. Morgan's 2026 long-term capital market assumptions, benchmarked against the Norwegian Government Pension Fund and the Yale Endowment.

Key findings

  1. 01Academic research since Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986) has consistently found that strategic asset allocation explains the large majority of the variation in portfolio returns over time, more than security selection or market timing.
  2. 02The Efficient Frontier identifies the set of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk; the point where the Capital Market Line is tangent to the frontier — the Maximum Sharpe Ratio portfolio — is the theoretically optimal risky portfolio for any investor, regardless of risk tolerance.
  3. 03Because mean-variance optimization is highly sensitive to small changes in its return, volatility and correlation inputs, institutional investors widely use the Black-Litterman model to blend market-implied equilibrium returns with their own views, producing more stable and less concentrated allocations.
  4. 04An illustrative long-term portfolio built on J.P. Morgan's 2026 capital market assumptions (65% growth assets, 35% defensive assets) produces an expected return of 6.53%, volatility of 12.3%, a Sharpe Ratio of 0.28 and a beta of 0.81 — markedly more conservative than the Norwegian Pension Fund's 71.3% equity weight.
  5. 05Diversification benefits depend on assets being imperfectly correlated in the first place; because correlations between risky assets tend to rise during periods of financial stress, institutional investors supplement volatility-based risk measures with maximum drawdown, Value-at-Risk and stress testing.

Discussion

The paper synthesizes the foundational MPT literature (Markowitz, Sharpe, Lintner) with more recent institutional practice, including the Black-Litterman model and published capital market assumptions from J.P. Morgan, BlackRock, Vanguard and Morningstar, to connect portfolio theory with how allocators actually implement it.

The illustrative portfolio and its comparison against the Norwegian Government Pension Fund and Yale Endowment are included for educational purposes rather than as a specific recommendation; the paper explicitly frames Modern Portfolio Theory as a decision-making framework whose principles can be adapted to a wide range of objectives, risk tolerances and time horizons, rather than a single fixed allocation.
