Albanian Real Estate Market (2015–2026): An Investment Perspective

Published20 Jul 2026
Length24 pages
CategoryMacroeconomics
Download PDF

Abstract

Albania has experienced one of the strongest residential real-estate booms in Southeast Europe over the past decade, with house prices rising significantly faster than incomes, particularly in Tirana and the coastal regions. Drawing on data from the Bank of Albania, Instat, the IMF and the World Bank, this paper assesses whether that appreciation reflects sustainable fundamentals or growing imbalance. Unlike many historical housing booms, the market is not primarily credit-driven; instead, demand is increasingly supported by tourism, diaspora capital, urbanization and foreign investment. The overall assessment is a market that is moderately overvalued relative to domestic purchasing power, with a positive long-term outlook but moderate downside risk.

Key findings

  1. 01The nationwide Fischer house price index rose from 1.0 in 2015 to 3.6 by 2025 — a cumulative increase far outpacing wage growth, leaving Tirana buyers needing roughly 18 years of income to purchase an apartment, the 37th-highest ratio globally.
  2. 02Mortgage penetration remains moderate rather than excessive: bank financing covered an estimated 61% of property purchases by late 2025, and the market shows none of the highly leveraged characteristics of a classic pre-2008-style credit bubble.
  3. 03Roughly one-third of Albania's housing stock sits unoccupied (32.9% in the 2023 census, up from 11.3% in 2001), with vacancy rates as high as 52% in Vlora County, pointing to a meaningful share of stock held as investment or seasonal property.
  4. 04Real-estate FDI has risen from an estimated 16% of total FDI in 2019 to 24% in 2025, alongside record remittances (€2.27 billion in 2024) that increasingly fund cash purchases and reduce reliance on mortgage credit.
  5. 05The base-case scenario assigns a 50% probability to 4–6% annual house-price growth through 2030, a 25% chance of a stronger 7–10% cycle tied to EU-accession momentum, and a 25% chance of stagnation (-2% to +2%) if oversupply or weaker tourism demand materializes.

Discussion

The analysis combines house-price indices, mortgage-market data, demographic statistics and foreign-investment flows from four primary institutional sources to build a fundamentals-based view of the Albanian housing market, rather than relying on a single price series in isolation.

Because reliable, long-run transaction-level data is limited and Albania's large informal economy complicates income measurement, the scenario probabilities and overvaluation assessment reflect the author's own judgment applied to the available official data, and should be read as a structured framework for monitoring the market rather than a precise forecast.
