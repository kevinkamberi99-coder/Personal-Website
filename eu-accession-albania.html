Institutional Convergence and Economic Transformation: Assessing the Impact of EU Accession on Albania

Published15 Jun 2026
Length34 pages
CategoryMacroeconomics
Download PDF

Abstract

Albania enters the final phase of EU accession from a position of relative macroeconomic strength, with growth among the highest in Europe, contained inflation and declining public debt. This paper argues that Albania's greatest opportunity lies not in further macroeconomic stabilization but in strengthening the quality and effectiveness of its institutions. Drawing on the comparative experience of Slovenia, Croatia and Bulgaria, it finds little evidence that EU membership itself causes sustained inflation, and instead identifies judicial independence, procurement transparency and anti-corruption enforcement as the primary channels through which accession is likely to support long-term convergence.

Key findings

  1. 01The inflation experience of Slovenia, Croatia and Bulgaria shows no consistent pattern of accession-driven inflation; their largest price shocks occurred during global events — the 2008 financial crisis, the 2021–2023 energy crisis — years or decades after joining the EU, not at the moment of entry.
  2. 02Historically, GDP per capita in newly acceded countries has risen by an average of 27% following accession, equivalent to roughly 2 percentage points of additional annual growth versus a non-accession counterfactual.
  3. 03Albania's Worldwide Governance Indicators score remains well below EU averages on Control of Corruption (39.3 vs. 76.5), Voice & Accountability (56.6 vs. 78.5) and Rule of Law (54.9 vs. 80.5), marking institutional quality — not macroeconomic stability — as the binding constraint on convergence.
  4. 04Trade with the EU already represents 57% of Albania's total trade and 72.5% of exports, meaning accession's main economic benefit is expected to come from institutional and regulatory alignment rather than new tariff reductions.
  5. 05Key transition risks include accelerated emigration (45% of Albanian firms already report difficulty filling vacancies), pressure on the SME sector that employs 82.5% of the workforce, and reduced housing affordability as accession expectations draw in foreign and diaspora capital.

Discussion

The paper benchmarks Albania's macroeconomic indicators and Worldwide Governance Indicators scores against Croatia, Slovenia and Bulgaria at comparable stages of their own accession processes, using IMF Article IV data, World Bank governance indicators and European Commission progress reports as the primary sources.

Because Albania has not yet acceded, the analysis is necessarily forward-looking; it treats the experience of prior enlargement rounds as a comparative benchmark rather than a precise predictor, and explicitly separates macroeconomic effects — which the evidence suggests are limited — from institutional effects, which the paper identifies as the primary long-term channel of impact.
